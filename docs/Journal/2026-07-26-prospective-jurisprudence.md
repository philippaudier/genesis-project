# 2026-07-26 — Prospective jurisprudence

A short entry to fix a method that named itself today, during the review of DN-004.

## Jurisprudence has begun changing how we write

Principles are no longer proposed because they please. They are verified against the corpus, in a
fixed order: *does a precedent exist?* — then *does an invariant force it?* — and only then, *the
principle may stay.* DN-004's two reinforcements were of exactly these two natures: one historical
(the Source's state-versus-machinery ruling, applied as precedent to a new category), one logical
(invariant 6, forcing the conclusion). A good jurisprudence does not merely record decisions; it
changes how future ones are written. That has now demonstrably happened.

## The name

The second reinforcement deserved a name of its own, and received one: **prospective jurisprudence.**

Precedents look backward. Invariants hold now. Prospective jurisprudence looks forward: it asks for
the only form of a decision that can never become contradictory as the project grows —

> *Choose today the only shape that remains valid in every admissible future.*

Applied to DN-004: a Constraint is silent today, so its declaration could live anywhere without
consequence — but under *any* future answer to Q1 it will affect the world, and invariant 6 will then
demand it be declared state. Only one present choice survives all futures. Architectures usually
break because they optimise the present; this method selects for temporal compatibility instead. It
is close to a design-stability theorem — and it is the Constitution's day-one instinct (*"design
every decision as though it will still matter in ten years"*) turned from an aspiration into a test
that can be run.

There is a symmetry worth recording: this is the design-time analogue of what the kernel does at
runtime. The Snapshot Transition Model chooses each next state so that determinism holds under any
composition of laws; prospective jurisprudence chooses each next decision so that consistency holds
under any composition of futures. The project has started designing itself the way it simulates.

## The grammar

The week's summary, in one displacement: Genesis's vocabulary no longer extends by C# types. It
extends by **categories of statement** — observation, theorem, declaration, predicate, constraint,
policy. First structures, then primitives, now a grammar. The project is leaving the language of
programs for the language of disciplines — no longer only an engine trying to be correct, but a
project trying to make its own reasoning transmissible.

Nothing is opened by this entry. Q1–Q6 sleep in their catalogue. The world turns.
